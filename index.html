Mannequin
Bass

Ken Butler 2012

At first glance, Ken Butler’s Mannequin Bass might appear to be an interactive installation.

On closer observation, however, it begins to appear more like a sculpture.

Even though it remains completely still and does not produce sound in the gallery, it somehow still seems to be performing. The mannequin appears almost like a performer standing on a stage.

Through my research, I began to notice that the relationship between the human body and musical instruments has appeared repeatedly in art.

String instruments in particular already have a strong visual relationship with the body.

A cello is close to the scale of a person, its curved shape resembles the human figure, and playing it requires close physical contact.

Man Ray’s Le Violon d’Ingres, for example, visually transforms the human body into a string instrument. Butler takes this relationship further by physically combining an artificial human body with parts of an instrument.

A mannequin can feel cheap, artificial, and familiar because it is something we commonly see in stores.

However, when Butler combines it with parts of a cello or bass, it suddenly seems more elegant and unfamiliar.

Mannequin Bass is displayed silently and does not function as an interactive instrument. Yet its form still strongly refers to a musical instrument.

This raises a different question: how much does an object need to function like an instrument before we begin to see it as one?

body?

Sculpture?

Instrument?

Performance?

Instead of fitting clearly into one category, Mannequin Bass seems to exist between body, object, sculpture, and the visual language of a musical instrument.

This ambiguity is important because each category changes the way I understand the work. When I see it as a sculpture, I pay attention to its form and materials. When I notice its resemblance to an instrument, I think about how strongly its form suggests sound even though the work itself remains silent. When I see the mannequin as a body, the instrument-like elements begin to feel almost like an extension of that body.

The work becomes interesting precisely because it cannot be understood through only one of these categories. Its ambiguity keeps shifting the boundary between body and object, and between sculpture and instrument‑like form.
